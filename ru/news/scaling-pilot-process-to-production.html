Scaling a pilot extraction process to production without losing the profile

Scale-up failures are usually heat-transfer and mixing problems in disguise. A method for finding them before you commit capital.

AWA Process Engineering 21 августа 2025 г. 9 min read

A pilot process that produces an excellent extract is not evidence that the process will work at ten times the volume. Most scale-up failures we are called in to diagnose are not chemistry problems at all; they are heat-transfer and mixing problems that change the thermal history of the material and therefore the profile of the finished extract.

The three variables that usually move

  • Surface-area-to-volume ratio. A larger vessel has proportionally less jacket area, so the same jacket temperature produces a slower heat-up and a longer residence time at elevated temperature.
  • Mixing regime. Impeller tip speed and Reynolds number do not scale linearly with volume; a process that was turbulent in the pilot vessel can become transitional in production.
  • Residence-time distribution. Dead zones and bypassing in a larger vessel broaden the time the material spends at temperature, which is exactly what degrades heat-labile markers.

A practical sequence

  1. Characterise the pilot process as a thermal history, not as a set of setpoints. Record the actual temperature curve of the material, not the jacket.
  2. Identify the marker compounds whose degradation kinetics you care about, and measure them across a deliberately varied thermal history.
  3. Model the production vessel’s heat-transfer coefficient and mixing time from its geometry, then predict the thermal history at scale.
  4. Run a single confirmation batch at intermediate scale with instrumented temperature mapping before committing to full production.
  5. Define a design space with control limits, and validate at the edges of that space rather than only at the centre.
If your scale-up plan is "same recipe, bigger tank", the tank is not the problem you should be worried about.
