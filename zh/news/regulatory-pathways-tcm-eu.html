Regulatory pathways for TCM products entering the European Union

Traditional herbal registration, food-supplement routes and the national differences that decide which one you can actually use.

AWA Regulatory Affairs 2026年1月18日 8 min read

Entering the European Union with a traditional herbal product is less a single procedure than a choice between four routes, and the choice is usually made for you by the product’s form, claim and history of use. Getting this wrong is the most expensive mistake a new exporter makes, because reformulation after a rejected dossier can invalidate your stability and clinical work.

The four routes in practice

  • Traditional Herbal Medicinal Product registration (Directive 2004/24/EC) — requires 30 years of traditional use, 15 of them inside the EU. It permits traditional-use claims and is the strongest position for a finished herbal medicinal product.
  • Well-established use or full marketing authorisation — realistic only with substantial published or proprietary clinical evidence and a sponsor prepared for a multi-year process.
  • Food supplement notification — the pragmatic route for many botanical products, but it forbids medicinal claims entirely and is governed by national notification rules plus the novel food regulation for anything without a significant pre-1997 EU consumption history.
  • Food for special medical purposes or cosmetic — narrow, category-specific, and highly sensitive to labelling.

Where exporters lose time

Three issues account for most of the delay we see. First, novel food status: a botanical that is well known in Asia may still be novel in the EU, and the safety dossier for novel food authorisation is a serious undertaking. Second, monograph conformity: several member states assess herbal preparations against their own pharmacopoeial monographs, and a specification that satisfies one authority can fail another. Third, claim language: marketing teams write claims that would be lawful as food information and unlawful as a medicinal claim, and the translation into each market language is where the boundary is tested.

The cheapest regulatory work happens before the formulation is frozen, not after the first dossier is rejected.

What we do to de-risk this

  • Route assessment against your product form, intended claims and target member states, delivered as a written opinion rather than a verbal estimate.
  • Specification review against the relevant pharmacopoeial monograph and the strictest target market.
  • Novel food screening with a documented history-of-consumption evidence pack.
  • Label and claim review in each target language by a native-speaking regulatory reviewer.
  • Dossier assembly and, where you need it, introduction to notified bodies and legal representatives in the target state.

None of this removes the regulatory risk, and we will tell you when a product has no viable route at an acceptable cost. What it removes is the avoidable delay caused by discovering the route after the formulation, label artwork and stability programme are already committed.
